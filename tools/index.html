Tools of Astronomy

Almost everything we know about the stars comes from their light. Here is how we split it and how we collect it.

The spectrograph: a barcode reader for light

A spectrograph spreads light out by wavelength. Every element absorbs and emits at its own exact wavelengths, so the pattern of lines tells you what a star is made of, how hot it is, and how fast it is moving.

Light source

Elements in the gas

Mercury and neon are lab-lamp elements, used to calibrate spectrographs. Mercury is not seen in normal stars (only as ionised Hg II in rare hot "HgMn" stars), and neon shows only weak lines in hot B stars.

A hot solid glows at every wavelength whatever it is made of, so there are no element lines to add. Pick another source to use the elements.

In a star, line depths depend on temperature: hydrogen is strongest near 9,500 K, helium shows only above ~15,000 K, and calcium, sodium, magnesium and iron fade in hot stars. A lab lamp is thin gas lit up by an electric current, not heated like a star's atmosphere, so all its lines show whatever the temperature.

Kirchhoff's three rules

Hot, dense object → continuous spectrumA hot solid, liquid or dense gas glows at every wavelength. Its colour, and the wavelength of its peak (Wien's law, λpeak = 2.9 mm·K / T), give its temperature.
Hot, thin gas → bright emission linesElectrons dropping between energy levels give off photons of exact energies. Each element has its own set of lines, like a fingerprint. Nebulae and neon signs look like this.
Cool gas in front of a hot source → dark absorption linesThe cooler gas absorbs exactly the wavelengths it would emit. A star's outer layers do this to the light from below, which is why stellar spectra are covered in dark lines.

Common mistake

"Red-shifted stars look red." A redshift moves every line by the same fraction; it doesn't recolour the star. You measure it from where known lines land, not from the colour.

"The Doppler shift gives a star's full speed." It measures only motion along the line of sight. A star moving straight across the sky shows no shift at all; its sideways motion has to come from watching its position change over years.

Why gratings won

A prism spreads blue light more than red and absorbs some ultraviolet. A diffraction grating spreads wavelengths almost evenly, so wavelengths can be read off a straight ruler. Most research spectrographs use gratings.

Resolving power: how fine a spectrograph splits light

R = λ / ΔλΔλ is the smallest wavelength gap the instrument can still show as two lines. Sodium's pair at 589.0 and 589.6 nm is 0.6 nm apart, so splitting it needs R ≳ 589 / 0.6 ≈ 1,000. A school spectroscope manages a few hundred and shows one yellow line.
What sets RA narrower slit gives sharper lines (but lets in less light), and a grating with more lines lit, or used at a higher order, spreads the colours further. Research instruments trade light for resolution to suit the job.
Why it matters for speedThe smallest Doppler shift you can see directly is about c / R. ESPRESSO on the Very Large Telescope reaches R ≈ 140,000 (Δλ ≈ 0.004 nm at 550 nm, about 2 km/s per resolution step); fitting thousands of lines at once then pins a star's speed far below that.

How it was done

In the 1920s Cecilia Payne used spectra like these to show that stars are mostly hydrogen and helium. Strong lines don't simply mean "lots of that element": temperature decides which lines can form.

Tip

Hydrogen's Balmer lines (656, 486, 434, 410 nm) are widely spaced and get closer toward blue. Sodium is a tight pair at 589 nm. Magnesium is a triplet near 517 nm. Calcium's H & K pair (393, 397 nm) comes from ionised calcium (Ca II) and stays strong into A stars; the 423 nm line is neutral Ca I and only shows in cool stars. Temperature also gives the spectral type (hot to cool: O B A F G K M): ~20,000 K is a B star, ~9,500 K an A star, ~6,000 K the G/F border, ~4,500 K a K star.

What can a telescope see?

Pick a telescope, or set your own. Aperture (the diameter of the main lens or mirror) is what matters most.

Binoculars have built-in eyepieces, so the magnification is fixed (8× here).

Targets (green = within reach, faded = too faint or too fine)

    Rules of thumb: faintest star ≈ 7.7 + 5 log10(D in cm) magnitudes; resolution ≈ 116″ / D(mm) (Dawes limit, for equal double stars); highest useful magnification ≈ 2 × D(mm). Seeing (air turbulence) usually limits ground telescopes to ~1–2″. Space telescopes and adaptive optics escape that limit. Plate scale = 206.265 × pixel(μm) / focal length(mm) arcseconds per pixel.

    Common mistake

    "More magnification means a better telescope." Magnification is just focal length ÷ eyepiece, so any telescope reaches 500× with a short enough eyepiece. Above about 2× the aperture in mm you only enlarge the blur: try the 80 mm refractor with a 3 mm eyepiece (200×, over its useful 160×).

    Also wrong: "longer focal length = bigger telescope"

    A 102 mm Maksutov-Cassegrain has a 1,300 mm focal length, longer than the 8″ Dobsonian's 1,200 mm, yet the Dobsonian collects about 4 times more light and resolves twice as fine. Focal length sets the image scale; aperture sets how much light and detail you get. (Keck's primary alone is f/1.75, 17.5 m; its instruments work at f/15, about 150 m.)

    Why every big telescope is a reflector

    No colour fringesA mirror reflects every wavelength the same way. A lens bends blue more than red (chromatic aberration), so the colours focus at different points.
    Support from behindA lens can only be held at its edge, so a big one sags under its own weight. A mirror can be supported across its whole back, or built from segments.
    One surface, any glassLight never passes through a mirror, so the glass needn't be flawless inside, and only one surface must be polished. The largest refractor ever used for research, Yerkes (1897), has a 1.02 m lens; segmented mirrors now reach 10.4 m (Gran Telescopio Canarias), and 25–39 m telescopes are being built.
    Light-gathering grows as D²Double the aperture and you collect four times the light. A 200 mm telescope gathers ~800 times more than your 7 mm pupil.
    Resolution grows as DDiffraction blurs every point into a small disk of angular size ≈ 1.22 λ / D. Bigger apertures, or interferometers linking dishes across the Earth, see finer detail.
    Magnification matters leastAny telescope can magnify a lot; above about 2× the aperture in mm you only enlarge the blur. Light-gathering and resolution set what you can actually see.

    Beyond visible light

    Each kind of light needs its own kind of telescope.

    Radio: dishes and interferometersRadio waves are long, so a dish only has to be smooth to a small fraction of the wavelength; some are wire mesh. Linking dishes far apart gives the sharpness of one dish as wide as their separation. The Earth-sized Event Horizon Telescope resolves about 20 millionths of an arcsecond.
    Infrared: keep it coldAnything warm glows in infrared, including the telescope itself. Webb shades its mirror to below about 50 K (−223 °C) and cools its mid-infrared camera to about 7 K. Water vapour in the air blocks much infrared, so these telescopes go high or into space.
    X-rays: grazing mirrorsX-rays go straight into an ordinary mirror. They only bounce at grazing angles of about a degree, so X-ray telescopes such as Chandra nest long, barrel-shaped mirrors one inside another. The air blocks X-rays, so they must fly in space.
    Gamma rays: no mirrors at allGamma rays can't be focused. Fermi's Large Area Telescope lets each one turn into an electron–positron pair in thin tungsten sheets, then tracks the pair through silicon detectors to work out where the gamma ray came from.
    Electronic camerasModern CCD and CMOS sensors record around 90% or more of the photons that reach them, against a few per cent for photographic film, and give a measured number for every pixel. That is the same gain as making the mirror 4–6 times wider.
    Adaptive opticsA guide star, real or a laser spot in the upper air, shows how turbulence is distorting the light. A thin deformable mirror reshapes itself up to about a thousand times a second to undo it, giving large ground telescopes like Keck nearly diffraction-limited infrared images.

    Current research

    ESO's Extremely Large Telescope in Chile (39 m, 798 mirror segments) aims for first light in 2029. The Giant Magellan Telescope (25.4 m, seven 8.4 m mirrors, also in Chile) is being built but is not yet fully funded. The Thirty Meter Telescope's future is unclear: the US National Science Foundation backed only the Giant Magellan in 2025, and in September 2026 TMT's partners turned down Spain's offer (€400 million, later a €1 billion package with loans) to build it on La Palma, leaving contested Mauna Kea as its only site. Rubin Observatory began its 10-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time in late June 2026, imaging the whole southern sky every few nights.

    Measuring tiny wobbles

    Spectrographs such as ESPRESSO on ESO's Very Large Telescope aim to measure a star's speed to 10 cm/s, close to the 9 cm/s wobble the Earth gives the Sun. That takes an instrument held at extremely steady temperature and pressure, calibrated against ultra-stable light sources such as laser frequency combs.
