Missions & Telescopes

The machines that showed us the universe: what light each one sees, what its detectors are, where it flies, and what it found.

Filter by the light they see

Every kind of light carries different news. Pick a band of the spectrum to see which missions observe it, then open any mission for its instruments and discoveries.

Band edges are conventions, and fields draw them in slightly different places: millimetre waves are “microwave” to some astronomers and “radio” to others.

    The atmosphere is a filter

    Air lets through visible light, some infrared and radio, and almost nothing else. To see X-rays, gamma rays, most ultraviolet and far-infrared light, you must get above it.

    Why those places

    Low Earth orbit (~500 km)Cheap to reach and, for Hubble, reachable by astronauts for repairs. But Earth blocks half the sky and the telescope swings from sunlight to shadow every 95 minutes.
    Sun–Earth L2 (1.5 million km)A balance point beyond Earth, opposite the Sun. Sun, Earth and Moon all stay on one side, so one shield keeps an infrared telescope cold and dark.
    Sun–Earth L1The balance point between Earth and the Sun: a non-stop view of the Sun and a station for measuring the solar wind before it hits us.
    Earth-trailing orbitCircling the Sun while drifting slowly away from Earth. Far from Earth's heat and glare; the cost is a radio link that gets weaker every year.
    Elongated Earth orbitsChandra, XMM-Newton and TESS spend most of each orbit far out, above the radiation belts, with long stretches of uninterrupted viewing.
    Going thereSome things can't be seen from far away: the inside of a Martian rock, the gas in a plume, the magnetic field of interstellar space. Probes measure them on the spot.

    Common mistake

    “L2 is a fixed parking spot.” L2 is a balance point, not a place to sit still, and it is unstable: anything there slowly drifts off. Webb, Gaia and the others loop around it in wide halo orbits hundreds of thousands of km across, firing thrusters every few weeks to stay on track.

    Why not exactly at L2

    Seen from the point itself, Earth would cover most of the Sun’s disk and cut the solar power. A halo orbit keeps the Sun in view and Earth in radio contact.

    Mirrors to scale

    The main mirror sets how much light a telescope collects (it grows with the area) and how sharp it can see (finer detail with a larger diameter, at a given wavelength).

    Resolution = 1.22 λ / D (the diffraction limit), before any blurring by air or imperfect optics. Resolution only matters where a telescope detects light, so rows fade outside each one’s band. A longer wavelength needs a bigger mirror for the same sharpness: why Webb, working in infrared, needed 6.5 m. Chandra (grazing mirrors) and Gaia, Planck and WMAP (non-round mirrors) are left out.

    Light collected compares areas like for like: Hubble ~4.0 m² once the shadow of its secondary mirror is removed (Roman, with the same 2.4 m mirror, is close), Webb 25.4 m². Other rows use the full mirror area, so they are slightly high.

    Common mistake

    “Telescopes are about magnification.” Any telescope can magnify; an eyepiece or software does that. What a bigger mirror buys is more light (fainter objects) and finer resolution (real detail). Magnifying past the resolution limit just enlarges the blur.

    Common mistake

    “Webb replaced Hubble.” They see different light: Hubble works from the ultraviolet to the near-infrared, Webb from red light out to the mid-infrared. They often observe the same targets to cover both, and both are still working.

    Common mistake

    "Space photos show the true colours." Most detectors record brightness only. Colour images are built from several exposures through filters, and infrared or X-ray images are mapped to visible colours chosen by scientists.

    Why cold matters

    Warm objects glow in infrared. An infrared detector at room temperature would be blinded by its own heat, so Webb's mid-infrared camera is cooled to about 7 K and Planck's bolometers to 0.1 K.
