        Why it stops at iron

        Binding energy per nucleon. Moving up the curve releases energy. Light nuclei fuse uphill; heavy nuclei split uphill. Both meet at the iron peak.

        Making gold and platinum

        Fusion stops at iron. Everything heavier is built by neutron capture: a nucleus absorbs neutrons (no electric repulsion to fight), then a neutron turns into a proton by β⁻ decay, moving it up one element.

        Spacetime and proper time

        Mass curves spacetime, and objects follow the curves. Your chapter 24 slide shows the key idea: the Sun and the white dwarf it will become have almost the same mass, but the white dwarf's well is deeper. Here every object sits on one shared well, measured in its own Schwarzschild radius rs. Outside, the wells are identical; what changes is how far down you can go before you hit the surface.

        time normaltime slowest

        dτ/dt = √(1 − rs/r)  outside · rs = 2GM/c²

        Inside a star the app uses the uniform-density interior solution, dτ/dt = 1.5√(1 − rs/R) − 0.5√(1 − rsr²/R³). Real stars are denser at the centre, so interior values are approximate.

        Does time stop at the event horizon?

        Only for someone watching from far away. A clock dropped from rest at 10 rs into a 10 M☉ black hole, shown both ways:

        Far away

        The falling clock ticks slower and slower and its light shifts red and fades. It seems to freeze just outside the horizon and never cross. dτ/dt → 0 there.

        On the clock

        Its own time never slows for it. It crosses the horizon after a finite time, notices nothing special, and reaches the singularity a fraction of a millisecond later. No clock can hover at or inside rs, so "the clock's rate there" has no meaning.

        Simplification: the far view uses Schwarzschild coordinate time and ignores the extra delay for light to travel out.

        Light climbing out of a gravity well loses energy and shifts red: 1 + z = 1/√(1 − rs/R).

        *cz is shown only for comparison with Doppler shifts; it is not a real velocity, and the comparison breaks down for large z like the neutron star's.

        GPS has to correct for it

        Satellites orbit ~20,000 km up, where the gravitational potential is ~4× shallower (gravity itself is ~17× weaker; clock rate follows the potential). Without the correction, positions drift ~11 km (7 miles) per day.

        Where it starts: the equivalence principle

        Inside a closed rocket accelerating at 1 g you cannot tell, by any experiment, that you are not sitting on Earth. A light beam crossing the accelerating cabin hits the far wall lower, so light must also bend in gravity. Einstein built general relativity from this idea (section 24.1).

        Supermassive black holes

        Sgr A* at the centre of the Milky Way: ~4 million M☉, weighed from the 16-year orbit of the star S2 (Nobel Prize 2020). The Event Horizon Telescope imaged M87* (2019) and Sgr A* (2022). Bigger black holes have lower average density inside the horizon.

        Gravitational waves: LIGO's chirp (GW150914)

        Two black holes (~36 and ~29 M☉) spiral together. Frequency and amplitude rise (the chirp) until merger, then the new ~62 M☉ black hole rings down. About 3 M☉ c² left as gravitational waves. The waveform here uses the leading-order chirp formula f(τ) ∝ τ−3/8.

        Common mistake

        "Black holes suck everything in." At 10 rs, a black hole pulls exactly like a star of the same mass. If the Sun became a black hole, Earth's orbit would not change. Only close in (inside ~3 rs) does gravity behave differently.

        Common mistake

        "The EHT photographed the event horizon." It imaged the glowing gas and photon ring around the shadow. The horizon itself emits nothing and is smaller than the shadow.

        Real black holes, same picture

        Everything scales with rs = 2GM/c², so this image fits any non-spinning black hole. Only the sizes change.

        rs = 2GM/c² · photon sphere 1.5 rs · shadow bc = (3√3/2) rs ≈ 2.6 rs · ISCO 3 rs · disk T ∝ r−3/4(1 − √(rin/r))1/4 · g = √(1 − 1.5rs/r) / (1 − ΩL)

        Simplifications: no spin (real holes spin, which shifts the ISCO and flattens one side of the shadow), a geometrically thin disk with no jet, and light from the disk only (no glowing atmosphere). Ray paths use the exact Schwarzschild orbit equation.

        Clusters: watching stars age

        One star changes too slowly to watch. A cluster gives us thousands of stars born together with different masses, so the heavy ones age first. Drag the age and watch the main sequence peel away from the top. The point where it bends off, the turnoff, tells you the cluster's age (chapter 22).

        Newborn clusters still have low-mass stars above the main sequence (still contracting). Old clusters have lost their massive stars and show a red giant branch and white dwarfs. Blue stragglers look too young for their cluster: they gained mass from a partner or merged, which is common in crowded globular clusters where stars are ~1 light-year apart.

        The model uses solar composition. Real globular clusters such as Omega Centauri are metal-poor (Population II), so their helium-burning stars spread into a bluer horizontal branch where RR Lyrae live.

        The distance ladder

        Every distance in astronomy rests on the one below it (chapter 19). Radar measures the Solar System, which gives the AU. The AU is the baseline for parallax. Parallax calibrates the standard candles. The root of everything is trigonometric parallax.

        1 · Radar

        Radio waves travel at c. Time the echo, halve it.

        2 · Trigonometric parallax

        1 parsec (parallax of one arcsecond) = 3.26 light-years. Air blurs tiny angles, so Hipparcos and Gaia measure from space.

        3 · Standard candles

        Know how bright it really is (L), measure how bright it looks, and the inverse-square law gives the distance. Henrietta Leavitt found the Cepheid period–luminosity law in 1912.

        The Leavitt law

        Longer period means brighter Cepheid. RR Lyrae all have about the same luminosity whatever their period (hours to a day), so they need no period at all.

        4 · Spectroscopic parallax: distance from the H-R diagram

        No parallax is involved. The spectrum gives the star's type and luminosity class (V dwarf, III giant, I supergiant), which places it on the H-R diagram and so gives its true brightness (section 19.4).

        Gas, dust and the birth of stars

        The interstellar medium is the raw material (chapters 20–21). Only about 1% of a molecular cloud becomes stars.

        Emission nebula (H II region)

        Hot O and B stars ionize hydrogen; it glows red (H-alpha) as electrons recombine. Orion Nebula, Rosette.

        Reflection nebula

        Dust scatters starlight. Blue scatters most, so it looks blue. The Pleiades' wisps.

        Dark nebula

        Dense dust blocks light behind it: Barnard 68 (a Bok globule), the Horsehead, the Coal Sack. Infrared sees through.

        Molecular cloud

        Cold (10–20 K), dense H₂ with dust. Stellar nurseries. Mapped by CO and infrared.

        Dust dims and reddens starlight

        true colour

        what we see

        Like a morning fog: extinction dims, and because blue is scattered more, the star looks redder than it is. Not the same as redshift.

        Protostars on the H-R diagram

        Low-mass protostars start cooler but much brighter than they will be on the main sequence, then drop down (Hayashi track) and slide left (Henyey track). Massive stars contract in only ~10⁵ years and are still hidden in dust when they ignite.

        Seeing cold hydrogen: the 21-cm line

        In a neutral hydrogen atom the electron and proton spins can be parallel or opposite. Flipping from parallel to opposite releases a tiny photon at 21.1 cm (1,420 MHz). One atom waits ~10 million years, but there is so much H I that radio telescopes map it across the Galaxy, and its Doppler shift traces the spiral arms.

        Cosmic rays

        Not rays at all: charged particles moving at nearly light speed. About 90% are protons, ~9% helium nuclei, the rest heavier nuclei and electrons.

        Most are accelerated by supernova shock waves in our Galaxy. When one hits the upper atmosphere it shatters a nucleus and starts a shower of secondary particles (pions, muons, neutrinos) that reaches the ground.
