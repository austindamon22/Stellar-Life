Black holes

Places where gravity wins completely. How big they are, how they feed and merge, what quantum physics says happens at their edge, and which of these ideas have actually been tested.

A region light cannot leave

Squeeze any mass into a small enough ball and the speed needed to escape from its surface exceeds the speed of light. The boundary of no return is the event horizon. For a black hole that does not spin, its radius is the Schwarzschild radius, rs = 2GM/c²: about 3 km for each mass of the Sun. Double the mass and the size doubles. For the view up close, ray-traced, see the black hole camera on the Stars page.

Spin a* = cJ/(GM²) runs from 0 (not spinning) to 1 (the fastest a horizon can turn); the slider stops at 0.998, about the most that gas falling in can spin a hole up (Thorne 1974). Radii are Boyer–Lindquist radii for a Kerr black hole, from Bardeen, Press & Teukolsky (1972). Gas spiralling in gets as close as the innermost stable orbit before it plunges, so a spinning hole lets it release more energy.

Spaghettification

Gravity pulls harder on the part of you that is closer. Falling feet first, your feet are pulled more than your head, and the difference (the tidal stretch) grows as 1/r³. At the horizon it scales as 1/M²: the bigger the black hole, the gentler its edge.

Stretch between head and feet: Δa ≈ 2GM·L/r³ for a body of length L = 2 m, Newtonian estimate (close to the exact value for a radial fall). The drawing exaggerates the stretch so you can see it. "Torn apart" here means above about 100 m/s² (10 g) of stretch, a rough line; bodies differ.

Close encounters

Tidal disruption: a star torn apartA star that strays too close to a supermassive black hole is stretched into a stream. About half of it is flung away; the rest falls back and lights up a flare that outshines its galaxy for months, then fades. ASASSN-14li (2014, about 290 million light-years away) is a well-studied example; AT2018hyz launched a radio outflow years after its flare. Each galaxy has one roughly every 10,000 to 100,000 years. Above about 100 million M☉, a Sun-like star crosses the horizon before tides can rip it, so it is swallowed whole with no flare.
Watching someone fall inSeen from far away, a falling probe slows as it nears the horizon, its light stretched redder and dimmer until it fades from view. It seems to freeze at the edge. That is an effect on the light, not on the probe: its own clock shows it crossing the horizon, and reaching the centre, in a finite time (for a 10 M☉ hole, well under a millisecond from the horizon).
Frame dragging and the Penrose processA spinning hole drags space around with it. Inside the ergosphere, which reaches 2 GM/c² at the equator, nothing can stay still relative to distant stars: everything is swept along with the spin. There, a particle can split so that one piece falls in with negative energy and the other escapes with more than it brought (Penrose 1969). Up to 29% of a maximally spinning hole's mass is rotational energy that can be taken out this way. Magnetic fields threading the horizon can tap the same energy (Blandford & Znajek 1977), the leading explanation for the most powerful jets.

How black holes form, and what they keep

Collapse of a massive starWhen a star of more than roughly 20 solar masses runs out of fuel, its iron core (about 1.5 Sun masses, the size of Earth) collapses in under a second. If too much falls back for a neutron star to hold up, a stellar-mass black hole forms, sometimes with no visible supernova at all.
Direct collapse, and mergersIn the early universe, a cloud of pristine gas may have collapsed straight into a black hole of 10,000 to 100,000 Sun masses, a head start for supermassive ones (a hypothesis with some supporting hints, such as the X-ray quasar UHZ1). Black holes also grow by merging, as LIGO hears.
Primordial black holes (hypothesis)Dense patches in the first second after the Big Bang might have collapsed into black holes of almost any mass (proposed by Zel'dovich and Novikov in 1967, and Hawking in 1971). None has been found. Searches with lensing and the cosmic background rule them out as all of dark matter over most mass ranges.
The no-hair theoremOnce it settles, a black hole in general relativity is described by just three numbers: mass, spin and electric charge (which is near zero in nature). Every other detail of what fell in is radiated away or hidden. "Black holes have no hair," as John Wheeler put it.
Dark, but not invisibleWe find black holes by what they do: stars orbiting nothing visible (Sgr A*, Gaia BH3), X-rays from gas swallowed in a binary (Cygnus X-1, identified in the early 1970s), radio rings (EHT) and gravitational waves.
The singularityGeneral relativity predicts that matter inside ends at a point of infinite density. Most physicists read this as the theory breaking down, not as a real infinity. A quantum theory of gravity is needed to say what is really there. See Quantum black holes.

Common mistake

"Black holes suck everything in." Far from it, a black hole pulls exactly like any other object of the same mass at the same distance. If the Sun were replaced by a 1 M☉ black hole, Earth would keep the same orbit (and freeze). Gas only falls in after losing its orbital motion, which is why it piles up in a disk first.

Why it matters

What is special is how close you can get. A black hole is so compact that its gravity near the horizon is strong enough to bend light into orbits (at 1.5 rs for no spin) and to make circular orbits unstable (inside 3 rs).

Feeding: the brightest things in the universe

A black hole emits no light of its own. But gas spiralling toward it rubs against itself, heats to millions of degrees and glows, outshining every star in its galaxy. A supermassive black hole caught doing this is an active galactic nucleus (AGN); the brightest of them are quasars. Viewing angle explains much of the zoo of names; play with that on the Galaxies page's active galaxies tab. Here we go inside the engine.

How fast can it eat?

Light pushes on the infalling gas. At the Eddington luminosity, LEdd = 4πGMmpc/σT ≈ 1.26 × 1031 W × (M/M☉), that push balances gravity on ionised hydrogen. Pick a mass, a brightness and a spin.

Efficiency η = 1 − EISCO: the share of rest-mass energy gas has lost by the time it reaches the innermost stable orbit; 5.7% at no spin, 32% at a* = 0.998 (where radiation from the disk is thought to cap spin up, Thorne 1974) and 42% in the limit a* = 1. Mass needed: Ṁ = L/(ηc²). Disk temperature from the thin-disk model (Shakura & Sunyaev 1973), a rough guide. Growth time at the Eddington limit: the mass grows e-fold every (η/(1−η)) × 450 million years.

Common mistake

"The light comes from the black hole." Nothing escapes the horizon. The light comes from gas outside it, heated as it falls: most from the inner disk, a few times rs out, and the rest from the corona, clouds, dust and jets, light-hours to light-years away.

Why it matters

Falling into a black hole is the most efficient way known to turn mass into light: up to 60 times better than the fusion that powers stars (0.7%). That is how a region the size of the Solar System can outshine a galaxy.

The quasar era, and black holes that grew too fast

Bright quasars were most common about 10.5 to 11.5 billion years ago (z ≈ 2–3), when galaxies had the most gas. JWST now finds black holes much earlier than expected. Choose one to test it: could a black hole born at z ≈ 30 grow that big in time, eating at the Eddington limit with η = 10%?

Common mistake

"A quasar is a jet." Most quasars have no strong jet: only about one in ten is radio-loud. A quasar is defined by its disk-powered brightness. A jet pointed at us makes a blazar, which can be a quasar or a much weaker AGN.

What we know now

The "little red dots" JWST finds in the first billion years show broad hydrogen lines like AGN, but are weak in X-rays and barely flicker. As of late 2026 there is no consensus: dust-hidden black holes, or black holes inside a dense gas envelope ("black hole stars"), with masses possibly much lower than first estimated.

When black holes collide

Two black holes in orbit stir spacetime and send out gravitational waves, which carry away energy. The orbit shrinks, the holes speed up, and the waves rise in pitch and loudness: a "chirp". Then they merge, and the new black hole rings like a struck bell before going quiet. On 14 September 2015, LIGO heard one for the first time.

Hawking's area theorem, heard

In 1971 Hawking proved that, in classical general relativity, the total horizon area can never shrink. A merger is the test: the new hole is lighter than the two together (energy left as waves), yet its horizon must be bigger. For a Kerr black hole, A = 8π(GM/c²)²(1 + √(1 − a*²)).

GW250114 (detected 14 January 2025, signal-to-noise ratio about 80, the clearest yet) confirmed the area increase at up to about 4.4σ, depending on the analysis, and its ringdown showed two tones that both match one Kerr black hole of 62.7 M☉ and spin 0.68 (LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA 2025).

Three ways to listen

LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA: 10 Hz to a few kHzLaser interferometers with arms 3 to 4 km long, sensitive to stellar-mass mergers. The fourth observing run (O4) ended on 18 November 2025 with about 250 candidate signals, on top of 90 from earlier runs. A six-month run (IR1) is planned to begin in late October or mid-November 2026, not yet confirmed as started (checked 12 Nov 2026); dates for O5 are being reassessed.
LISA: 0.1 mHz to about 1 HzThree spacecraft 2.5 million km apart, adopted by ESA (with NASA) in January 2024 for launch around 2035. It will hear supermassive black holes of 104 to 107 M☉ merging anywhere in the universe, and small objects spiralling into big black holes, mapping spacetime around them.
Pulsar timing arrays: nanohertzPulsars are precise clocks. A wave lasting years makes them run fast and slow in a pattern that depends on the angle between them (the Hellings–Downs curve). In June 2023, NANOGrav, EPTA, PPTA and CPTA reported evidence for this background hum, most likely from pairs of supermassive black holes. Not yet a 5σ detection.

Common mistake

"Merging black holes make a huge explosion of light." Two black holes in empty space merge in the dark. GW150914 radiated about 3 Sun masses of energy, more power at its peak than all the stars in the visible universe, but all of it as gravitational waves. Light came with GW170817 because neutron stars have matter to throw out (a kilonova).

Why it matters

The ringdown is the cleanest test of the no-hair theorem: every tone of the ringing must match the same mass and spin. So far they do.

Quantum black holes: not quite black

In 1974 Stephen Hawking combined quantum field theory with the curved spacetime of a black hole and found that it should glow, with a temperature inversely proportional to its mass. This is a firm prediction of the theory, but it has never been observed: for real black holes it is far too faint. Tags on this tab say how sure each idea is.

Hawking temperature and lifetime Theory, not observed

TH = ħc³/(8πGMkB) ≈ 6.17 × 10−8 K × (M☉/M). The cosmic microwave background bathes everything at 2.725 K, so any black hole colder than that absorbs more than it emits and grows. Choose a mass.

Blackbody estimate (photons, no greybody factors): lifetime t ≈ 5120πG²M³/(ħc⁴) ≈ 2.1 × 1067 years × (M/M☉)³ and power P = ħc⁶/(15360πG²M²) treat the hole as a perfect blackbody sphere of radius rs radiating photons only. The full calculation (Page 1976) differs in two ways: greybody factors (part of each wave is reflected back in by the curved spacetime, which lowers the output) and extra particle species (gravitons, neutrinos and, for hot holes, electrons, quarks and gluons, which raise it a lot). Together they make small holes die faster: one evaporating about now started near 5 × 1011 kg, not the blackbody figure (Carr et al. 2010). Entropy S = kBA/(4ℓP²) with ℓP = √(ħG/c³) = 1.6 × 10−35 m (Bekenstein 1973, Hawking 1975).

The vacuum depends on who is looking

In quantum field theory, "particle" means a wave of a definite frequency, and "frequency" needs a clock. In flat spacetime everyone agrees on the clock, so everyone agrees on the empty vacuum. When spacetime changes, the waves that meant "no particles" before mean "some particles" after. Leonard Parker found this for the expanding universe in 1968; Hawking's 1974 result is the same effect for a collapsing star.

Bogoliubov coefficients, in wordsWrite the "after" wave as a mix: α parts of a positive-frequency wave plus β parts of a negative-frequency one. If you started with no particles, you now find |β|² particles in that mode, created in pairs. A sudden change makes many; a slow, gentle change makes exponentially few. The bottom wave shows it: when β ≠ 0, the two parts beat and its envelope wobbles.
The model used hereA massive field in a two-dimensional universe whose size changes as C(η) = 5 + 4 tanh(ρη), one of the few cases solved exactly (Bernard & Duncan 1977; Birrell & Davies 1982). The wave is integrated step by step; the exact |β|² is shown alongside as a check.
Why changing spacetimes are a research frontierHawking's calculation uses a collapse, but then idealises it. What a real, messy, time-dependent spacetime produces (a collapsing star, two holes merging, a ringing remnant, the hole shrinking as it radiates) is still worked out case by case, often numerically. These are open problems where a careful calculation can still be new.
From this toy |β|² to Hawking's thermal glowHere the change is a smooth step, so |β|² falls off quickly with frequency. Near a forming horizon the change never stops: a wave leaving from just outside the horizon is stretched (redshifted) by a factor that grows exponentially in time, eκt, where κ = c³/(4GM) is the surface gravity. Tracing a late outgoing mode back to the collapse squeezes it into an exponentially short wavelength. That exponential mixing gives exactly |β|² = 1/(eħω/kT − 1) with kT = ħκ/(2π): the Planck spectrum at the Hawking temperature (Hawking 1975). Try it in the Hawking calculator above.

Acceleration has a temperature too Theory, not observed

An observer accelerating through empty space should feel a warm bath of particles at T = ħa/(2πckB) (Unruh 1976; Fulling 1973, Davies 1975). An inertial observer sees nothing. Same vacuum, different clocks.

The information paradox and the Page curve Untested idea

Hawking radiation is thermal: it seems to carry no memory of what fell in. If a black hole evaporates completely, the information would be destroyed, which quantum mechanics forbids. Don Page (1993) showed what the entanglement entropy of the radiation must do if information escapes: rise, then fall back to zero. Move through the black hole's life.

Simplified: entropy of the radiation counted as equal to the black hole entropy lost (the real ratio, for photons and gravitons, is about 1.48: Page 1976, 1983), and the hole's entropy falls as (1 − t/tlife)2/3.

Islands and replica wormholes (2019)Two groups (Penington; Almheiri, Engelhardt, Marolf & Maxfield) showed that a careful semiclassical calculation of the radiation's entropy finds an extra contribution, an "island" inside the horizon, after the Page time. It gives the Page curve. "Replica wormholes" in the gravity path integral explain why. A major result, but in simplified models.
What is settled, what is notMost theorists now expect information to come out. How it is encoded in the radiation, and what an infalling observer sees, is not settled. Nothing here has been tested by experiment; the evidence is mathematical consistency.
Bekenstein–Hawking entropyA black hole's entropy is a quarter of its horizon area in Planck units: about 1077 kB for one solar mass, far more than the star that made it. Area, not volume, suggests that gravity stores information on boundaries, an idea called holography.

Common mistake

"Hawking radiation is a particle–antiparticle pair split at the horizon." That is Hawking's own cartoon, not his calculation. The calculation compares field modes before and after the collapse (Bogoliubov coefficients, as above). The radiation emerges from a zone a few rs across around the hole, and its typical wavelength is about 10 to 20 times the hole's size (the Wien peak sits near 16 rs), far too long to come from a pair split at one point.

Why it matters

It is the one place where gravity, quantum theory and thermodynamics meet in a single formula, TH = ħc³/(8πGMkB). Any quantum theory of gravity must reproduce it, and the entropy A/4.

How big does a shadow look?

For a non-spinning black hole, the shadow is √27 GM/c² in radius, about 2.6 times the horizon. Its angular size is that diameter over the distance. An Earth-sized radio telescope at 1.3 mm wavelength resolves about 20 μas (microarcseconds). For M87*, the bright ring the EHT images is about 10% larger than the shadow edge computed here, a factor calibrated with simulations; for Sgr A* the two agree within the errors.

Classic picture

Black holes were a mathematical curiosity: Schwarzschild's 1916 solution, which Einstein himself doubted described anything real.

What we know now

We have photographed two, tracked stars around one for 30 years, and heard hundreds collide. Everything measured so far fits general relativity's Kerr black holes. The surprises, if any, are expected at the quantum scale, far below what we can see.
