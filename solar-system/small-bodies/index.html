Small bodies

Asteroids, comets and the icy worlds beyond Neptune: leftovers from the making of the planets. What they are, where they orbit, and what happens when one hits Earth.

The asteroid belt: a planet that never formed

Between Mars and Jupiter, from about 2.1 to 3.3 AU from the Sun (1 AU is Earth's distance), orbit more than a million rocks wider than 1 km. Jupiter's gravity stirred them up early on, so they hit each other too fast to stick together. Collisions and Jupiter have since thrown most of the material out. What remains adds up to only about 3% of the Moon's mass.

The Kirkwood gaps

Count asteroids by the size of their orbit (semi-major axis) and gaps appear. They sit where an asteroid would go round the Sun a whole number of times while Jupiter goes round a smaller whole number: 3 laps to Jupiter's 1, for example. Pick a distance on the chart, or a resonance below.

Why a gap formsIn the 3:1 resonance, an asteroid meets Jupiter at the same points of its orbit every 3 laps. The small tugs add up instead of cancelling, the orbit gets more and more stretched, and within a few million years it crosses Mars or Earth and is removed. Many meteorites come from here.
Daniel Kirkwood, 1866With fewer than 100 asteroids known, the American astronomer noticed that none had periods a simple fraction of Jupiter's. The gaps carry his name.
Resonances can also protectNot every resonance clears space. The Hilda asteroids gather at the 3:2 resonance (3.97 AU), and the Trojans share Jupiter's orbit (1:1), clustered 60° ahead of and behind it at the L4 and L5 points. Both are kept safely away from Jupiter by the same timing. NASA's Lucy probe is on its way to visit Trojans from 2027.

Common mistake

"You can see the gaps in a picture of the belt." Not in a snapshot. Asteroid orbits are stretched (eccentric), so an asteroid with a 2.5 AU orbit spends time well inside and outside 2.5 AU. Turn on the resonance rings in the top view: no empty lanes. The gaps only show when you sort asteroids by orbit size.

Why it matters

Resonances act as escape hatches. They feed a steady trickle of asteroid pieces into orbits that cross Earth's, which is why we have meteorites from the belt at all, and where most near-Earth asteroids come from.

Where the mass is

The whole belt has a mass of about 2.4 × 1021 kg (Pitjeva & Pitjev 2018, from their pull on the planets and spacecraft). Four bodies hold more than 60% of it. Choose one.

What asteroids are made of

Sunlight reflected off an asteroid carries the fingerprints of its minerals. Sorting by these colours and spectra gives the main types. Of asteroids classified this way, roughly three quarters are C-type and about a sixth S-type.

CCarbon-richDark as charcoal, reflecting only 3–10% of sunlight. Clay minerals with water locked in them, and organic compounds. Most common in the outer belt. Samples from Ryugu (Hayabusa2, 2020) and Bennu (OSIRIS-REx, 2023) are of this kind.
SStonyBrighter, made of silicate rock with some metal. Common in the inner belt. Grains brought back from Itokawa by Hayabusa in 2010 matched ordinary chondrites, the most common meteorites.
MMetal-richSome look like iron–nickel, perhaps the exposed cores of small worlds whose rocky outsides were stripped by collisions. NASA's Psyche probe, launched in 2023, arrives at 16 Psyche in 2029 to find out.

Common mistake

"The belt is a crowded field of rocks you must dodge." Space between asteroids is vast: on average they are around a million kilometres apart, more than twice the Earth–Moon distance. More than a dozen spacecraft have crossed the belt without trouble. Standing on one asteroid you would usually not see another with the naked eye.

Why it matters

Films show a dense rock field because a realistic belt would look empty. The true number is large (over a million bodies wider than 1 km), but the volume is far larger: a ring about 1.2 AU wide around the whole Sun.

Comets: dirty snowballs on stretched orbits

A comet's nucleus is a lump of ice, dust and rock a few kilometres across. For most of its orbit it is frozen and dark. As it nears the Sun, ice turns straight to gas, dragging dust with it into a glowing cloud (the coma) and two tails that can stretch for tens of millions of kilometres.

Ion tailStraight and blueGas molecules are ionised by sunlight and swept up by the solar wind, which blows outward at about 400 km/s. So this tail points straight away from the Sun. The blue is glowing CO⁺ ions.
Dust tailCurved and paleDust grains are pushed gently by the pressure of sunlight but keep much of the comet's orbital motion, so they fan out in a curve that lags behind. It shines by reflected sunlight, so it looks yellowish-white.

Common mistake

"A comet's tail streams out behind it, like a rocket's exhaust." The tails point away from the Sun, whichever way the comet moves. Heading out after perihelion, a comet travels tail-first.

Why it matters

The ion tail is a free wind sock. Before any spacecraft measured it, Ludwig Biermann used comet tails in 1951 to argue that a steady wind of particles flows out from the Sun: the solar wind, confirmed by spacecraft in 1959–1962.

Anatomy of a comet

Water ice starts to sublimate (turn straight from solid to gas) at about 3 AU from the Sun, inside Jupiter's orbit. More volatile ices, such as carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide, can switch a comet on farther out.

Nucleus: kilometresThe solid part. Halley's is about 15 × 8 km, 67P's about 4 km, Hale–Bopp's unusually large, perhaps 60 km. It is darker than coal: Halley's reflects about 4% of the light that hits it. A crust of dust covers the ice.
Coma: up to a million kmThe cloud of gas and dust around the nucleus. Near the Sun it can grow wider than Jupiter, from a nucleus smaller than a city. Around it, a still larger cloud of hydrogen glows in ultraviolet.
Tails: up to 100 million km+Hyakutake's ion tail (1996) was measured at over 500 million km by the Ulysses spacecraft, which flew through it by chance. Most tails are a few tens of millions of km long.

What visiting comets taught us

1986 · Giotto at HalleyESA's probe flew 596 km from Halley's nucleus on 14 March 1986, taking the first pictures of a comet's core. It showed a dark, peanut-shaped body with jets of gas from a few active spots, not a clean snowball.
2005–2006 · Deep Impact and StardustDeep Impact fired a 370 kg copper block into comet Tempel 1, revealing fine powdery material under the crust. Stardust brought dust from comet Wild 2 to Earth, and it held minerals that formed at high temperatures close to the young Sun, then travelled out to the comet region.
2014–2016 · Rosetta at 67PESA's Rosetta orbited comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko for two years, and its lander Philae touched down on 12 November 2014. Its water has about three times the heavy-hydrogen (D/H) ratio of Earth's oceans, so comets like it probably did not supply most of Earth's water. It also found molecular oxygen and the amino acid glycine.

Classic picture

Fred Whipple's 1950 "dirty snowball": a ball of ice with some dust mixed in.

What we know now

Spacecraft found more dust than ice: an "icy dirtball". 67P's nucleus is very porous, about 70% empty space, with a density close to half that of water ice (about 0.53 g/cm³). It is two lobes stuck together, like many small bodies.

The Kuiper belt and the Oort cloud: the far, frozen edge

Past Neptune lies the Kuiper belt, a wide ring of icy bodies, Pluto among them. Far beyond, a vast round shell of comets, the Oort cloud, is thought to reach a good part of the way to the nearest stars. Distances out here span so many powers of ten that only a logarithmic scale can show them together.

Common mistake

"Voyager 1 has left the Solar System." In August 2012, at about 122 AU, it left the heliosphere, the bubble blown by the solar wind, and entered interstellar space. But the Sun's gravity still rules far beyond: Voyager will take about 300 years to reach the inner edge of the Oort cloud and perhaps 30,000 years to pass through it.

A milestone this month

Around 18 November 2026, Voyager 1 becomes the first spacecraft one light-day from Earth, about 173 AU or 25.9 billion km. A command sent from Earth then takes a full day to arrive, and the reply another day to come back.

Pluto and Neptune: crossing orbits that never meet

Pluto's orbit dips inside Neptune's, yet they can never collide. Pluto goes round the Sun twice for every three of Neptune's (a 3:2 resonance), timed so that whenever Pluto is closest to the Sun, Neptune is far away around the other side.

Frame

Flat model: Neptune on a circle at 30.07 AU, Pluto on an ellipse with a = 39.5 AU and e = 0.249, with the period ratio set to exactly 3:2 and the timing at the centre of its slow swing (libration). In reality Pluto's orbit is also tilted 17°, which adds to the separation. The small dot marks Pluto's perihelion, just inside Neptune's orbit; it was last there in 1989.

PlutinosPluto is not alone: thousands of Kuiper belt objects share the same 3:2 resonance, near 39.4 AU. They are called plutinos. Their orbits suggest Neptune migrated outward early on and swept them into step.
1992 QB1, the firstDavid Jewitt and Jane Luu found the first Kuiper belt object after Pluto and Charon in 1992, after five years of searching. Thousands are now known, and there are likely over 100,000 wider than 100 km.
ArrokothOn 1 January 2019, New Horizons flew past Arrokoth, 6.6 billion km from the Sun. It is a reddish snowman 36 km long: two lobes that came together gently, at walking pace, left untouched for 4.5 billion years since the planets formed.

Classic picture

Nine planets, with Pluto, found by Clyde Tombaugh in 1930, as the ninth. For decades it was thought to be about Earth's size; it turned out to be smaller than our Moon.

What we know now

On 24 August 2006 the IAU defined a planet as a body that (1) orbits the Sun, (2) is massive enough for its gravity to pull it nearly round, and (3) has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. Pluto passes the first two but shares its zone with the rest of the Kuiper belt, so it became a dwarf planet. The trigger was Eris, found in 2005: about Pluto's size and 27% more massive.

SednaIts closest approach to the Sun is about 76 AU, well beyond Neptune's reach, and it swings out to around 900 AU. Something else must have shaped its orbit: perhaps a star passing near the young Sun. A few similar bodies are now known.
The Oort cloud is inferred, not seenNo Oort cloud object has been observed out there; they are too small and faint. In 1950 Jan Oort noticed that long-period comets fall in from all directions and from distances of tens of thousands of AU, and deduced a huge round reservoir of maybe hundreds of billions of comets.
Where it endsBeyond roughly 100,000 to 200,000 AU, the pull of passing stars and the Milky Way's tides beats the Sun's. Those same nudges send Oort cloud comets falling inward, which is how we ever see them.

Impacts: when a small body hits Earth

Something from space hits Earth's atmosphere every day, mostly dust and sand. Because it arrives at 11 to 72 km/s, even a small rock carries enormous energy: at 20 km/s, each kilogram carries about 50 times the energy of a kilogram of TNT. Whether that energy makes a crater or an explosion in the sky depends mainly on size and what the body is made of.

Common mistake

"A crater is about the size of the rock that made it." An impact at many km/s is an explosion, not a dent. Craters come out 10 to 20 times wider than the impactor, and round even when it hits at a slant.

Small rocks burst in the air

Stony bodies up to a few tens of metres are crushed by the air in front of them and usually explode high up, as at Chelyabinsk and Tunguska. The danger is the blast wave and heat, not a crater. Iron bodies are much stronger and can reach the ground, as at Meteor Crater.

Chelyabinsk, 15 February 2013A stony asteroid about 19 m across hit the air at 19 km/s and burst about 30 km above Chelyabinsk, Russia, with the energy of roughly half a megaton. The shock wave blew in windows across the city, and about 1,500 people were hurt, mostly by flying glass. Nobody saw it coming: it arrived from the direction of the Sun.
Tunguska, 30 June 1908An explosion a few kilometres above Siberia flattened about 2,000 km² of forest, some 80 million trees. No crater was found. Estimates of its energy range from about 3 to 15 Mt, from a body several tens of metres across.
Chicxulub, 66 million years agoA body about 10 km wide hit what is now Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, leaving a crater about 180 km across now buried under sediment. Dust, soot and sulfur blocked sunlight around the world, and about three quarters of species, including all non-bird dinosaurs, died out.

Planetary defence: nudging an asteroid

On 26 September 2022, NASA's DART spacecraft, about 580 kg, hit Dimorphos, the 150 m moon of the asteroid Didymos, at 6.1 km/s. Its orbit period dropped from 11 h 55 min by 33 ± 1 minutes. A small change, but it builds up every orbit. Compare where Dimorphos would have been with where it is.

Sizes and orbit to scale with each other: Didymos about 760 m wide, Dimorphos about 150 m, orbiting 1.2 km apart. The shrink of the orbit is worked out from the period change with Kepler's third law.

More push than the spacecraftRock thrown off by the impact recoiled like a rocket exhaust. It gave Dimorphos about 3.6 times the push of the spacecraft alone (Cheng et al. 2023), good news for using this method on a real threat.
Hera, arriving nowESA's Hera, launched in October 2024, is due to reach Didymos in November 2026 to measure Dimorphos's mass and the shape of DART's crater, turning one experiment into a calibrated method.
Find them firstA nudge only works years ahead of an impact. Surveys have found well over 90% of near-Earth asteroids wider than 1 km, and none is on a collision course. Smaller ones, of a few hundred metres, are still largely undiscovered; NASA's NEO Surveyor telescope is being built to find them.

Meteors and meteorites: space dust meets the air

On any clear, dark night you can see a few meteors an hour. On certain dates each year, many more appear, all seeming to fly out of one spot in the sky. These meteor showers happen when Earth ploughs through a trail of dust left along a comet's orbit.

Three words, three places

Meteoroid
A small body in space, from about 30 µm (a speck of dust) up to 1 m across (IAU definition, 2017). Bigger than that is an asteroid or comet.
Meteor
The streak of light, not the object. Air rams into the meteoroid so hard that it heats up and vaporises, making a glowing trail of hot gas, mostly 120 to 70 km up. A typical shower meteor comes from a grain smaller than a grain of rice.
Meteorite
A piece that survives the fall and lands. Only bodies bigger than a few centimetres and slow enough leave meteorites; shower meteors, from fragile comet dust, essentially never do.
Fireball and bolide
A fireball is a meteor brighter than Venus. A bolide is one that explodes in a bright flash, often with a sonic boom heard minutes later.

Common mistake

"Shooting stars are burning stars" or "meteorites land red-hot." Meteors are grains of rock, burning nothing like a star; the light is heated air and vapour. Surviving meteorites lose their cosmic speed tens of km up and fall the rest of the way at a few hundred km/h. The heated outer skin ablates away, and they usually land no more than warm to the touch.

Why it matters

Most meteoroids are ancient material that never became part of a planet. Meteorites are the only pieces of asteroids, and of Mars and the Moon, that come to us without a space mission.

4,567 million yearsThe oldest solids in the Solar System are calcium–aluminium-rich inclusions (CAIs), white specks in some chondrites. Lead–lead dating puts them at 4,567.3 ± 0.2 million years (Connelly et al. 2012). That is the start of the clock for the Solar System's age.
ChondrulesThe little stone beads that give chondrites their name were once molten droplets, flash-heated and cooled within hours, over the first few million years. What heated them is still debated.
Finds versus fallsAmong meteorites simply found on the ground, irons are far more common than among falls: they look unusual, resist weathering and set off metal detectors. Deserts and Antarctic ice, where dark stones stand out, provide most finds today.
