Earth

The one planet we can study from the inside. How it is built, why its surface keeps moving, and what water, air and the Moon do to it.

Inside Earth: layers found by listening to earthquakes

The deepest hole ever drilled reached 12 km, about 0.2% of the way to the centre. Everything below that comes from earthquake waves: how they speed up, slow down, bend, bounce and sometimes vanish on their way through the planet.

P waves squeeze and stretch rock and can cross liquids. S waves shake it sideways and cannot. Each ray bends as the speed changes, following Snell's law in spherical shells: r · sin(angle) / speed stays the same all along the ray.

Waves

Rays are traced through PREM in 2 km shells. Only rays that start downward are drawn; real seismograms also show reflections off the surface and the core, and waves that change type (an S wave can enter the core as P and come out as S). Waves that just graze the core diffract around it, so real recordings see faint P out to about 104°.

1906 · Richard OldhamNoticed that waves crossing the far side of the planet arrived late. Something in the middle was slowing them down: the first sign of a core.
1914 · Beno GutenbergUsed the P-wave shadow to put the edge of the core at about 2,900 km depth, within a few tens of km of today's 2,891. In 1926 Harold Jeffreys argued it is liquid, since S waves never get through.
1936 · Inge LehmannSpotted faint P waves arriving inside the shadow zone, where none should be. She explained them with a smaller inner core, where waves travel faster and are bent back out. It was later shown to be solid.

Earth as a planet

4.54 billion years oldEarth's age comes from radioactive dating of meteorites, leftover building blocks from the same time, together with the oldest lead on Earth: 4.54 billion years, give or take about 1%. The oldest pieces of Earth itself are zircon crystals from the Jack Hills of Western Australia, about 4.4 billion years old.
Not quite roundSpin makes Earth bulge at the equator. Its equatorial radius is 6,378 km and its polar radius 6,357 km, a difference of 21 km. So the summit of Chimborazo in Ecuador, near the equator, is the point farthest from Earth's centre, not Everest.
A warm planet insideAbout 47 terawatts of heat flows out through Earth's surface, about two and a half times the rate at which humanity uses energy. About half comes from the decay of uranium, thorium and potassium in the rock, measured partly by detecting neutrinos from those decays (KamLAND 2011). The rest is heat left over from Earth's formation and the core slowly freezing.

Hot, crushed and solid

Why the inner core is solidIt is hotter than the outer core, yet frozen. The pressure there is 330–360 GPa, over 3 million times the air pressure you feel. That squeezing raises iron's melting point faster than the temperature rises with depth, so the core freezes from the centre outward.
Heavy in the middleEarth's average density is 5.5 g/cm³, but surface rocks are only 2.7–3. The extra mass has to be deep down. Iron cores of 10–13 g/cm³ make the numbers work, and they match iron meteorites.
Gravity peaks at the coreGoing down, you leave mass above you, but the dense core keeps the pull strong. In PREM, gravity rises to about 10.7 m/s² at the core–mantle boundary, then falls to zero at the centre.

Common mistake

"The mantle is an ocean of molten magma." It is solid rock: S waves, which cannot cross liquids, travel right through it. Over millions of years it flows like very stiff putty, a few cm per year.

Why it matters

Magma forms only in small pockets, where pressure drops, water is added, or the rock is unusually hot. That is why volcanoes sit at ridges, above sinking plates and over hotspots, not everywhere.

Open questions

Two continent-sized blobs at the base of the mantle. Under Africa and the Pacific, seismic waves slow down in two huge regions above the core, called large low-velocity provinces (LLVPs). Nobody knows for sure what they are. One idea (Yuan et al. 2023, Nature) is that they are dense, iron-rich remains of Theia, the body whose impact made the Moon, still sitting there after 4.5 billion years.

Still argued about

Does the inner core spin at its own pace? Repeating earthquakes suggest it turns slightly faster than the mantle at some times and slower at others. Yang & Song (2023) found the difference paused around 2009; Wang et al. (2024, Nature) found it slowed and began to slip backwards relative to the mantle. Other seismologists read the same signals differently. Waves that bounce across the planet up to five times also hint at an innermost inner core, about 650 km in radius, with different crystal alignment (Pham & Tkalčić 2023).

Plate tectonics: a surface that keeps moving

Earth's cold outer shell, the lithosphere, is broken into about fifteen large plates and many small ones. They move a few centimetres a year, about as fast as fingernails grow. Almost every earthquake, volcano and mountain range sits where two plates meet.

Speeds are measured with GPS and from sea-floor ages. "Full spreading" is how fast the two sides of a ridge move apart.

The stripes that proved sea-floor spreading

Earth's magnetic field flips every so often. Lava cooling at a ridge locks in the direction of the field at that moment, then spreads away on both sides. A ship towing a magnetometer reads the result as a barcode, mirrored around the ridge.

What moves the plates

Slab pullOld ocean floor is cold and denser than the mantle under it. Where it sinks at a trench, its weight drags the rest of the plate along. Plates with sinking edges move fastest.
Ridge pushNew plate at a ridge sits high because it is hot. It slides gently downhill away from the ridge as it cools and thickens.
Mantle convectionHeat from the core and from radioactive elements keeps the solid mantle slowly churning. The plates are the cold top layer of that circulation.

Classic picture

In 1912 Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift. The coastlines fit, and matching fossils and old glacier scratches lined up across oceans. Most geologists rejected it: he had no believable force that could push continents through solid ocean floor.

What we know now

Continents don't plough through anything. They ride on plates of lithosphere that glide over the soft asthenosphere, pulled mainly by sinking slabs. Sea-floor stripes in the 1960s, then GPS, which now measures each plate's motion directly, settled it.

Volcanoes: why some ooze and some explode

Whether a volcano pours out rivers of lava or blasts ash into the stratosphere depends mostly on one thing: how much silica is in its magma. Silica makes magma sticky, and sticky magma traps gas.

Viscosity is a rough trend at each magma's eruption temperature; heat and dissolved water each change it by orders of magnitude. Water flows at 0.001 Pa·s; honey is around 10.

VEI rates an eruption mostly by the volume of ash and rock it throws out, and by how high the column rises. Above VEI 2, each step is ten times more material.

Where volcanoes form, here and elsewhere

Mid-ocean ridgesMost of Earth's lava erupts here, unseen, building new sea floor. Pressure drop melts the rising mantle. Iceland is a ridge that rises above the sea.
Subduction zonesMost volcanoes on land, including the Pacific "Ring of Fire", sit above sinking plates, where water from the slab melts the mantle. Their silica-rich magma makes them the explosive ones.
HotspotsPlumes rising from deep in the mantle punch through the middle of plates: Hawaiʻi, Yellowstone, Réunion, Galápagos.
IoJupiter's moon is the most volcanic place in the solar system, with about 400 active volcanoes. Tides from Jupiter flex it and heat its interior, not radioactivity.
Olympus Mons, MarsA shield volcano like Mauna Loa but about 22 km high and 600 km wide. Mars has no moving plates, so the volcano stayed over its magma source and kept growing.
Ice volcanoesOn Saturn's moon Enceladus, jets of water vapour and ice spray from cracks near the south pole, fed by a hidden ocean. Cassini flew through them. Neptune's moon Triton has geysers too.

Common mistake

"Lava is what kills people." Lava flows are usually slow enough to walk away from. Most volcano deaths have come from pyroclastic flows (avalanches of hot ash and gas), lahars (volcanic mudflows), and the famine and disease after large eruptions.

Why it matters

In 1985 a fairly small eruption (VEI 3) of Nevado del Ruiz in Colombia melted summit ice. The lahars that followed swept down river valleys and buried the town of Armero, tens of kilometres away. They killed more than 23,000 people.

Water: the part of the planet that never sits still

Earth is the only planet with liquid water on its surface today. Sunlight lifts it as vapour, gravity brings it back down, and on the way it dissolves rock, carries sand downhill and lays it down as new layers. Most landscapes you see were shaped by water.

How water takes rock apart

Mechanical weathering breaksWater seeps into cracks, freezes and expands by about 9%, wedging rock apart a little more each winter. Roots, salt crystals and heating and cooling do the same. The pieces are smaller but chemically unchanged.
Chemical weathering dissolvesRain picks up CO₂ and becomes a weak acid. It dissolves limestone (making caves) and slowly turns feldspar in granite into clay. Warm, wet climates weather fastest, and the more cracks there are, the more surface the water can reach.
Sediment does the cuttingClear water barely scratches solid rock. Sand and boulders dragged along the bed during floods grind it down. That is why a river cuts fastest when it carries the most sediment.
Grand Canyon: old rock, young canyonThe dark rocks at the bottom (the Vishnu basement rocks) are about 1.7 to 1.8 billion years old. The Colorado River cut the canyon itself, up to about 1.8 km deep, in roughly the last 5 to 6 million years, as the Colorado Plateau rose.
Mars had rivers tooJezero crater on Mars holds a fan-shaped river delta, built where a river ran into a crater lake more than 3.5 billion years ago. The Perseverance rover landed there in 2021 because a delta sorts and buries fine mud, the best place to look for signs of past life.
Deltas sort grains by sizeWhere a river meets still water it slows suddenly. Gravel drops first, then sand, and the finest clay drifts farthest out. The diagram above shows why: each grain size settles at its own speed.

Common mistake

"The Grand Canyon is as old as its rocks." The rocks record almost 2 billion years of history, but the canyon cut through them is less than 0.5% of that age. Rocks are dated by when they formed; landscapes by when they were carved.

Why it matters

Erosion is fast on geological timescales. The continents lose a few centimetres per thousand years on average, which would flatten today's mountains in tens of millions of years if plate tectonics weren't lifting new ones.

A thin blanket and an invisible shield

If Earth were an apple, the atmosphere would be thinner than its skin: 99% of the air lies below about 30 km. That thin layer keeps the surface warm, burns up meteors and blocks ultraviolet light. Farther out, the magnetic field turns aside the solar wind.

The greenhouse balance

A planet warms until it sends out as much infrared as the sunlight it absorbs. A layer of gas that absorbs infrared sends part of it back down, so the surface has to get warmer to balance. Change the numbers.

Te = [S(1 − a) / 4σ]1/4
Tsurface = Te · [2 / (2 − ε)]1/4

What the model leaves out

One layer, one temperature, no clouds, no weather, no oceans moving heat. It gets Earth's average about right only because ε is tuned to fit. Real warming depends on how the absorbing gases are spread through many heights.

Why Venus breaks it

Even at ε = 1 a single layer can only warm a surface by 19% (21/4). Venus has about 90 times Earth's surface pressure of CO₂, which acts like dozens of layers stacked up, so its surface reaches about 737 K although its albedo is 0.76.

AuroraParticles from the solar wind get into the magnetotail, are accelerated toward Earth, and run down field lines into the upper air near the magnetic poles. They hit oxygen and nitrogen 100 to 300 km up, which glow green (oxygen, about 100–250 km), red (oxygen, higher) and blue-violet (nitrogen).
Van Allen beltsTwo doughnut-shaped zones of trapped, fast particles found by Explorer 1 in 1958. The inner belt is mostly protons, roughly 1,000 to 10,000 km up; the outer belt is mostly electrons, roughly 13,000 to 60,000 km up. Spacecraft cross them quickly to limit radiation damage.
The field flipsThe field comes from liquid iron moving in the outer core. Every few hundred thousand years on average it reverses; the last full reversal was about 780,000 years ago. New sea floor records each flip as magnetic stripes (see Plate tectonics).
Mars lost its shieldMars had a global field about 4 billion years ago; its crust is still magnetised in places. When its smaller core cooled the field shut off. NASA's MAVEN orbiter measures the solar wind still stripping gas from the top of Mars's thin atmosphere today.

Common mistake

"The thermosphere, at over 1,000 K, would burn you." Temperature measures how fast the molecules move. Up there they are so few, about two trillionths of the density at the ground near 400 km, that they deliver almost no heat. A spacesuit's temperature is set by sunlight and shadow, not by the air.

Why it matters

The ozone layer warms the stratosphere by absorbing ultraviolet, and that warm lid stops most storm clouds rising above the tropopause, 8 to 18 km up depending on latitude. The temperature profile is not a detail; it decides where weather can happen.

The Moon, the tilt and the seasons

The Moon raises the tides, slows Earth's spin and has been drifting away for 4.5 billion years. Compared with its planet it is unusually large: a quarter of Earth's width, probably made in one giant collision. The last section turns to Earth's own tilted axis, which, not the distance from the Sun, gives us the seasons.

Tides

Seen from above the North Pole, with the Sun far off to the left. Move the Moon around its orbit and the hour of day, or play. The water bulges are hugely exaggerated: in the open ocean they are well under a metre high.

Common mistake

"The Moon pulls the water up toward it." Then there would be one bulge, not two. The Moon pulls the near side of Earth a little harder than the centre, and the centre a little harder than the far side. That difference across Earth stretches it along the Earth–Moon line, making a bulge on each side, so most coasts get two high tides a day.

Why the numbers

Tidal pull scales with mass ÷ distance³. The Sun is 27 million times more massive than the Moon but 390 times farther, so its tide is about 0.46 of the Moon's (the Moon's is 2.2 times stronger). They add at new and full moon (spring tides) and partly cancel at the quarters (neap tides).

Measured with lasersApollo astronauts and Soviet rovers left reflectors on the Moon. Observatories time laser pulses bouncing back to millimetres. The distance grows by about 3.8 cm a year, roughly as fast as your fingernails grow.
Why it drifts awayEarth spins faster than the Moon orbits, so friction drags the tidal bulges slightly ahead of the Moon. Their pull speeds the Moon up, lifting it into a wider orbit, and brakes Earth's spin.
Longer daysTidal braking alone would lengthen the day by about 2.3 ms per century. Ancient eclipse records show about 1.8 ms; the gap is mostly Earth's shape rebounding since the ice ages. Tidal layers in 620-million-year-old rock (Williams 2000) record a 21.9-hour day and about 400 days a year.
Tidal lockingEarth's tides on the young Moon braked its spin in the same way, until it turned once per orbit. That is why we always see the same face. It does have a far side, not a dark side: every part gets two weeks of daylight per month.

Common mistake

"Phases are Earth's shadow on the Moon." Earth's shadow only reaches the Moon during a lunar eclipse, which can happen only at full moon. A crescent is simply the Moon seen mostly from its night side, with a thin edge of its day side showing.

Why not an eclipse every month?

The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to Earth's. Most months the Moon passes above or below the Sun–Earth line, so eclipses only happen in the few weeks a year when the line-up is exact.

Where the Moon came from

The leading idea: about 4.5 billion years ago a Mars-sized body, called Theia, struck the young Earth a glancing blow. Debris from both formed a disk around Earth that gathered into the Moon.

Matching isotopesOxygen, titanium and other isotopes in Apollo samples are almost identical to Earth's mantle. Meteorites from Mars and the asteroid belt all differ. The Moon is made of Earth-like stuff.
Too little ironThe Moon's density is 3.34 g/cm³, near that of Earth's rocky mantle. Its iron core is only 1 to 2 percent of its mass, against about a third for Earth. An impact would have thrown mostly mantle into orbit.
Once moltenPale highland rocks brought back by Apollo are anorthosite, which forms by floating on a sea of melted rock. The young Moon had a magma ocean hundreds of kilometres deep, which needs a lot of energy, fast.
Dried outLunar rocks are poor in elements that boil off easily, such as potassium and zinc, as if heated very hot. The large spin (angular momentum) of the Earth–Moon pair also fits a glancing blow.

Classic picture

A Mars-sized Theia hits Earth, and the Moon forms mostly from Theia's mantle (Canup & Asphaug 2001 style models). It explains the size of the Moon, its small core and the angular momentum.

What we know now

Measurements since 2001 show the Moon matches Earth too closely to be mostly Theia. Newer models mix the two far more: a faster-spinning early Earth, a more violent impact that left a vaporised cloud (a "synestia"), or a Theia already made of Earth-like material. Which is right is still open; that a giant impact happened is widely accepted.

Earth in orbit: why there are seasons

Earth's spin axis is tilted 23.44° to its orbit and keeps pointing the same way in space all year. So for half the year the north leans toward the Sun, and for the other half the south does. Change the tilt and your latitude, and pick a date, or click Earth on the orbit. The top diagram is the view at local noon, with the Sun far off to the left. Not to scale.

Sun's declination δ = tilt on the June solstice, −tilt in December, 0 at the equinoxes
noon Sun height = 90° − |latitude − δ|
between those dates δ ≈ tilt · sin(360° · (day − 79) ÷ 365)
cos H = −tan(latitude) · tan δ; day length = 2H ÷ 15° per hour

Day lengths are for the centre of the Sun on a flat horizon, without the bending of light by the air. Real sunrise to sunset is a few minutes longer.

Common mistake

"Summer is when Earth is closest to the Sun." Earth is closest (perihelion, about 147.1 million km) in early January, in the middle of northern winter, and farthest (aphelion, 152.1 million km) in early July. The distance changes by only about 3.3%, so the sunlight changes by about 6.9%. The seasons come from the tilt: it changes how high the Sun climbs and how long it stays up, and the two hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same time.

Why the tilt wins

At 40° north the noon Sun stands 73° high in June and only 27° high in December. Low sunlight is spread over more ground, about half as much per square metre at noon, and the December day is about 6 hours shorter. Together that gives a summer day roughly three times the sunshine of a winter day.

Milankovitch cyclesThe orbit and the tilt slowly change. The axis wobbles round once in about 26,000 years (precession); counting the orbit's own slow turn, the seasons line up with perihelion again every 19,000 to 23,000 years (about 21,000 on average). The tilt rocks between 22.1° and 24.5° over about 41,000 years. The orbit stretches and rounds out over about 100,000 and 405,000 years (eccentricity). Together they pace the ice ages.
Lines drawn by the tiltThe tropics, at 23.44° north and south, are as far from the equator as the Sun can stand straight overhead. The polar circles, at 66.56° (90° minus the tilt), are where the Sun can stay up for a whole day in summer and stay down for a whole day in winter. With no tilt the tropics would shrink to the equator and the polar circles to the poles, and every day of the year would be alike.
