Galaxies & Cosmology

The Milky Way, the galaxies beyond it, the monsters at their centres, and how they grew. How we know.

Our galaxy, from outside and inside

A barred spiral about 100,000 light-years across with a few hundred billion stars. We live inside its disk, 26,000 light-years from the centre, so we never see it face-on. Drag to fly around. Try From Earth to see why the Milky Way is a band across our sky.

Show

Population I: young, metal-rich, in the disk. Population II: old, metal-poor, in the halo, globular clusters and thick disk. Bulge and bar: old, but mostly metal-rich (near-solar or above).

The winding problem

Stars here orbit at the same speed at every radius (a flat rotation curve), so inner stars lap outer ones. If arms were made of the same stars, they'd wind up tight in a few hundred million years. Watch. (The bar turns as one solid piece, so it doesn't wind.)

The answer

Arms are density waves: slow traffic jams that stars pass through. Gas piles up in the jam, forms stars, and the brightest (short-lived blue) stars die before they leave it.

How we mapped a galaxy we're inside

Herschel's mistake (1785)Counting stars in every direction put the Sun near the centre of a small disk. Dust was hiding everything beyond a few thousand light-years.
Shapley's globular clusters (1918)Globular clusters sit in the halo, above the dust. Using variable stars to find their distances, Shapley saw they swarm around a point in Sagittarius, far from us: the Sun is not at the centre.
Radio and infrared see through dustThe 21-cm line of hydrogen (a flip of the electron's spin) passes through dust. Its Doppler shifts map the gas in the arms; infrared reveals the bar and the old stars.

How the Galaxy formed

Classic picture

One giant gas cloud collapsed all at once (Eggen, Lynden-Bell & Sandage, 1962). Halo stars formed on the way in; the rest settled into a spinning disk.

What we know now

It was built by mergers. Gaia's star motions revealed Gaia–Enceladus, a large dwarf galaxy that crashed in about 10 billion years ago; its stars still fill the inner halo. Today the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy is being torn apart into a stream of stars that wraps around the Milky Way.

    Common mistake

    The tuning fork is not a life story. Hubble himself wondered, but ellipticals don't flatten into spirals or the other way round. Galaxies change mainly by colliding and merging (see the Evolution tab).

    Common mistake

    "E7" doesn't mean a flat galaxy. The number is how flat it looks from Earth: E = 10(1 − b/a). A cigar-shaped elliptical seen end-on looks like an E0.

    The Great Debate, 1920

    Shapley argued the spiral "nebulae" were gas clouds inside our galaxy; Curtis argued they were separate galaxies. Neither could prove it.

    Settled, 1924

    Edwin Hubble found Cepheids in Andromeda and showed it is far outside the Milky Way (~900,000 ly by his calibration; 2.5 million ly today). The universe is full of galaxies.

    Weighing galaxies and the mass-to-light ratio

    Light tells you how many stars; motion tells you how much mass. The ratio says how much mass is hiding.

    The Sun has M/L = 1. Stars alone give M/L of about 1–10 (more for old red populations). Values far above that mean dark matter.

    One engine, many disguises

    Quasars, Seyfert galaxies, blazars and radio galaxies are the same thing: a supermassive black hole swallowing gas. What we call it depends mostly on the angle we see it from and whether it makes jets. Move the observer.

    What powers a quasar?

    Gas spiralling into a black hole can turn ~6–40% of its mass-energy into light (E = εmc²), far more than fusion's 0.7%.

    ε = 5.7% for a non-spinning hole (gas falls in from 3 rs), up to 42% for a maximally spinning one. Eddington limit LEdd ≈ 1.26 × 1031 W × (M/M☉): above it, radiation pressure pushes the gas away.

    How big is the engine?

    A quasar that changes brightness in a time Δt can't be much bigger than the distance light travels in Δt. Otherwise light from the far side would smear the change out.

    Classic picture

    Quasars outshine whole galaxies from a region the size of our Solar System.

    What we know now

    Nearly every large galaxy has a supermassive black hole, and its mass tracks the galaxy's bulge (the M–σ relation). Quasars are a phase that most big galaxies went through, mostly ~10 billion years ago.

    Quasars as backlights

    Reading the gas in betweenEvery gas cloud between us and a distant quasar absorbs some of its light at the cloud's own redshift, stamping narrow absorption lines on the quasar's spectrum. Hydrogen's Lyman-alpha line (121.6 nm) leaves a dense "forest" of them, one for each cloud along the way. This Lyman-alpha forest maps the hydrogen of the cosmic web across billions of light-years.

    Looking back in time

    Light from distant galaxies is old, so telescopes are time machines: the farther we look, the younger the galaxies. Hubble's law turns a redshift into a distance and an age.

    Redshift → lookback time

    Pick a redshift and see when the light left. Flat universe with Planck 2018 values: H0 = 67.7 km/s/Mpc, 31% matter, 69% dark energy.

    Common mistake: z = 7 does not mean 7 times the speed of light. v = cz only works for small z. At large z, redshift measures how much space has stretched while the light travelled: 1 + z = (size of universe now) / (size then).

    Galaxy collision

    Two disk galaxies swing past each other. Gravity's pull is stronger on the near side than the far side (tides), which flings out long tails of stars. Drag to rotate.

    Speed

    Tilt 0° = disk spins the same way the galaxies orbit (prograde): long tails. 180° = retrograde: almost no tails. Model: Toomre & Toomre (1972) restricted N-body, ~8,400 stars; real mergers also have gas that crashes, makes starbursts, and feeds the black holes.

    How galaxies grew

    Small first, big laterThe first galaxies were small, clumpy and blue. Big galaxies were built by merging many small ones ("hierarchical" growth).
    Cosmic noonStar formation across the universe peaked ~10 billion years ago (z ≈ 2), the same era quasars were most common. Today's rate is ~10× lower.
    Mergers make ellipticalsTwo spirals that merge scramble their orderly orbits into a puffy, random swarm: an elliptical. Older estimates said the Milky Way and Andromeda merge in ~4.5 billion years; a 2025 study with Gaia and Hubble data gives only about even odds within 10 billion years.
    The cosmic webGalaxies gather in groups, clusters and superclusters strung along filaments around huge voids. The Milky Way is in the Local Group, at the edge of the Virgo Supercluster (part of Laniakea).
    Dark matter is the scaffoldingGas collected in the dark-matter clumps first; galaxies formed inside them. Clusters hold ~5–10× more dark matter than visible matter.
    Lensing weighs clustersA cluster's mass bends light from galaxies behind it into arcs (gravitational lensing). The bending gives the total mass, dark matter included: far more than the stars and hot gas.
    What is dark matter?Not dim stars or black holes (MACHOs): microlensing surveys found far too few. Probably an unknown particle (WIMPs, axions), but every detector so far has come up empty.
    JWST surpriseThe James Webb Space Telescope finds bright galaxies already at z > 10, earlier and more of them than many models predicted. Active research.

    The Big Bang

    Not an explosion at a point in space, but space itself stretching everywhere at once, starting hot and dense 13.8 billion years ago. Three pieces of evidence: the expansion (Hubble's law), the cosmic microwave background, and the amount of helium.

    There is no centre

    Click any galaxy to stand on it. Arrows show how fast the others recede: always proportional to distance (Hubble's law), whichever galaxy you pick. Every observer sees themselves at the "centre", so there isn't one.

    Common mistake

    "The Big Bang happened somewhere." It happened everywhere: every point in today's universe was part of it.

    Common mistake

    "Galaxies fly through space." Mostly, space between them stretches. Galaxies themselves, and the Solar System, do not expand: gravity holds them together.

    Build a universe

    The future depends on what the universe contains. Matter's gravity slows the expansion; dark energy speeds it up. Curves are lined up at "today".

    5%27% DM68% dark energy

    Our universe (Planck 2018): 4.9% ordinary matter, 26.8% dark matter, 68.3% dark energy. Friedmann equation: (da/dt)² = H0²(Ωr/a² + Ωm/a + Ωk + ΩΛa²). Critical density ρc = 3H0²/8πG ≈ 8.6 × 10−27 kg/m³, about 5 hydrogen atoms per cubic metre; Ω is density divided by ρc.

    The cosmic background, cooling

    When the universe became transparent, it glowed at ~3000 K like a red-orange star. Expansion has since stretched that light 1,100 times longer into microwaves at 2.725 K. Slide back in time.

    Why it's evidence: the background is the most perfect blackbody ever measured (COBE, 1990), the same in every direction to 1 part in 100,000. A hot, dense early universe explains it; nothing else does.

    Ripples in the background

    The whole sky, flattened into an oval like a world map.

    The background's temperature varies across the sky by only about 1 part in 100,000 (tens of millionths of a kelvin). COBE first detected these ripples in 1992; WMAP and Planck mapped them in detail.

    They are the seeds of structure: slightly denser regions that gravity later grew into galaxies and clusters. The typical size of the ripples on the sky shows the universe is flat, and their pattern pins down its age and contents.

    Where the helium came from

    Stars can't have made it all: about a quarter of ordinary matter is helium everywhere, even in the oldest stars. It was made in the first few minutes.

    1. At 1 second, weak reactions stop: about 1 neutron per 6 protons (neutrons are heavier, so fewer).
    2. Free neutrons decay (half-life ~10 min) while the universe is still too hot for deuterium to survive, leaving ~1 : 7 by ~3 minutes.
    3. Then deuterium survives and almost every neutron ends up in helium-4. Fusion stops by ~20 minutes: too cool, too thin.

    Cross-checks and big questions

    The ages agreeThe oldest globular clusters are ~12–13 billion years old (from their main-sequence turnoffs, as in Stellar Life's Clusters tab), and the oldest white dwarfs ~12–13 billion. Both just fit inside the 13.8-billion-year age from the expansion. If any star were older than the universe, the model would be broken.
    Why is the universe so flat?Its density is within ~1% of critical. Any early difference would have grown hugely, so it must have started fantastically close. Inflation explains it by stretching any curvature flat.
    Why is there any matter?The hot early universe made matter and antimatter in pairs, and almost all of it annihilated into light. For roughly every billion pairs, one extra matter particle was left over: everything we are made of is that leftover. Known physics doesn't make enough of this imbalance; its origin is unknown.
    The anthropic principleMany constants seem tuned for life: a little different and there'd be no stable stars or no carbon. One view: we can only find ourselves in a universe that allows observers. Whether that explains anything is debated.

    The Drake equation

    Frank Drake's 1961 way of organizing our ignorance: N = R* × fp × ne × fl × fi × fc × L. The first three are now measured; the last four are guesses.

    Civilizations signalling in the Milky Way now

    Measured

    R* ≈ 1.5–3 stars per year. Kepler showed nearly every star has planets (fp ≈ 1), and roughly 10–40% of Sun-like stars have a rocky planet in the habitable zone.

    Unknown

    How easily life starts, whether it gets smart, and how long civilizations last could each be anything from ~1 to almost 0. The answer spans from "none but us" to millions.

    The Fermi paradox: if civilizations were common and long-lived, why haven't we seen any? Possible answers range from "life is rare" to "they don't last" to "we haven't looked enough". SETI has searched only a tiny fraction of stars and frequencies.

    Where to look in our Solar System

    MarsHad rivers and lakes ~3.5 billion years ago. Rovers find organic molecules and ancient lakebeds; any life today would be underground.
    EuropaJupiter's moon hides a salty ocean under its ice, kept liquid by tidal heating. NASA's Europa Clipper arrives in 2030.
    EnceladusSaturn's small moon sprays its ocean into space. Cassini flew through the plumes and found water, salts, organics and hydrogen: food for microbes on Earth.
    TitanLakes of liquid methane and a thick atmosphere; a water ocean deep inside. NASA's Dragonfly drone launches in 2028.
    Life's ingredients are commonThe Miller–Urey experiment (1952) made amino acids from simple gases and sparks. Meteorites carry amino acids; space is full of organic molecules.
    ExtremophilesEarth microbes live in boiling vents, acid, ice and rock kilometres down. Life's range is wider than "Earth-like" suggests.

    Common mistake

    "Near the habitable zone" or even in it doesn't mean habitable. Venus sits well inside the inner edge and is 737 K; Mars is inside the outer edge and frozen. The zone only says liquid water is possible on the surface with a suitable atmosphere.

    What we know now

    JWST reads exoplanet atmospheres. Claims of possible biosignature gases (e.g. on K2-18 b) have so far not held up to scrutiny; the hunt for oxygen plus methane together goes on.
